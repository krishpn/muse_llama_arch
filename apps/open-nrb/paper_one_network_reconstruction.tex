\begin{abstract}
Bilateral interbank exposures are rarely published, so studies of
contagion and spillovers reconstruct them from each bank's total
interbank lending and borrowing. We propose areconstruction that
combines a covariate-informed stochastic blockmodel, an amortized
recurrent inference network and a differentiable projection onto the
published totals and a per-pair regulatory exposure cap, solved by
Dykstra's alternating projections. On synthetic blockmodel networks with
known ground truth (24 banks, 16 periods, 5 seeds), the reconstruction
error is lower than that of the classical RAS method in every seed
(paired t-test p = 0.0101), but the latent block labels are not
recovered better than a majority guess (p = 0.1136). We then apply the
method to a hand-extracted monthly panel of 16 Nepali commercial banks
over 133 months. Here the published data, not the model, decide the
result. In 43 of 45 months of the clean window, total lending and total
borrowing within the 16 banks differ by more than 1\% (median relative
gap 0.32), so no non-negative matrix can match both totals; the
projection returns its least-violation compromise, and its miss tracks
the gap (Spearman 0.926). Two model fits whose latent posteriors end at
opposite degenerate states give matrices within a relative difference of
\(1.5	imes10^{-5}\). The cap binds for 1.9\% of entries in the clean
window. We conclude that the method works where its inputs are
consistent, and that Nepal's two published marginals do not support a
bilateral reconstruction: answering ``who is exposed to whom'' would
need bilateral or large-exposure reporting.
\end{abstract}

\section{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

Under Section 4 of the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058, a primary mandate of
the central bank is to maintain financial sector stability and build
public confidence in the banking system. Carrying out this statutory
objective requires regulatory tools that are capable of monitoring
systemic risk, and if in case of instability surfaces, tools to curtail
the contagion (Upper 2011). However, in the current setup the NRB eithr
does not publicly acknowledge the types of tools or techniques that it
employs to ascertain and contain the systemic risk or at least does to
measure the bilateral counterparty exposures. In this context,
envisioining and implementing a roubst and transparent framework beyond
the traditional regulatroy reporting, particularly that is capable of
capturing the bilateral exposure is crucial.

An example of paramount importance is provided by financial systems:
information on the interconnections between financial institutions is
privacy-protected, dramatically reduc- ing the possibility of correctly
estimating crucial systemic properties such as the resilience to the
propagation of shocks

Statutorily, Section 4 of the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058, mandatess the
central bank to maintain finsancial sector stability, sepcially by
monitoring interbanking expsoures.

Contagion analysis needs a network, and the network is usually not
observed. Central banks and researchers observe each bank's total
interbank lending and total interbank borrowing, and must reconstruct
the bilateral matrix whose row and column sums these are (Upper 2011;
Squartini et al. 2018). The classical answer spreads the totals as
evenly as possible (RAS or maximum entropy, Deming and Stephan (1940);
Gandy and Veraart (2013)), which is known to produce networks that are
too dense and to understate systemic risk where true bilateral data
exist for comparison (Mistrulli 2011; Anand, Craig, and Peter 2018).

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A capped, covariate-informed reconstruction.} We model the
  matrix with a stochastic blockmodel whose edge intensities depend on
  bank covariates, infer it with an amortized recurrent network, and
  force the output onto the published totals and a per-pair regulatory
  cap with a differentiable Dykstra projection. The cap is applied to
  each entry, as an exposure limit is, not to each row total; an earlier
  capped-Sinkhorn version that capped row totals is documented as a
  pitfall (Section 3.4).
\item
  \textbf{A synthetic test with honest scope.} With ground truth
  available, the method's reconstruction error is lower than RAS's in
  every seed. Its block labels are not better than a majority guess, so
  we make no claim about recovering core and periphery.
\item
  \textbf{A measured data limit on real data.} On Nepal's monthly
  bulletins, the two published totals do not balance within the observed
  banks in almost every month. We show what this does to any
  reconstruction that targets them: the misfit is set by the data, the
  projection sets the matrix, and the model's latent structure has no
  detectable effect on the output. For a regulator this is a concrete
  finding. The imbalance is first of all a sign that the 16 banks are
  not a closed system --- a panel bank can lend to or borrow from
  institutions outside the panel, so its two totals need not match
  within the panel even if every figure is correct --- and only possibly
  a sign that the two bulletin lines are defined inconsistently; we
  cannot separate the two from the published aggregates. Either way a
  bilateral reconstruction restricted to the panel is not identified
  from them.
\end{enumerate}

We report the third result as a finding, not a failure. Missing
bilateral data is the norm among central banks (Upper 2011;
\textbf{fsbimf2009infogaps?}), and data aggregation problems persist
even inside large banks (\textbf{bcbs2013principles?}). A method paper
that reports what real published data can and cannot support is useful
to the many emerging-market regulators who publish similar aggregate
returns. A companion paper (\textbf{paper2companion?}) uses the
reconstruction as an exposure weight in a study of Nepal's paid-up
capital mandate; we do not discuss causal questions here.

\section{2. Related work}\label{related-work}

\textbf{Matrix balancing and maximum entropy.} Iterative proportional
fitting (Deming and Stephan 1940; Stone 1961) rescales a prior matrix
until its margins match. Applied to interbank markets (Upper 2011; Gandy
and Veraart 2013), it gives the RAS baseline we use. Squartini and
Garlaschelli (2017) and Squartini et al. (2018) place these methods in a
maximum-entropy ensemble framework. Anand, Craig, and Peter (2018) show
that such ensembles spread exposures over too many links relative to
observed networks, and Mistrulli (2011), with Italian bilateral data,
shows how far maximum-entropy contagion estimates can be from those on
the observed network.

\textbf{Block structure and learned inference.} Interbank markets are
often tiered (Clementi, Gallegati, and Squartini 2019), which motivates
a stochastic blockmodel (Holland, Laskey, and Leinhardt 1983; Nowicki
and Snijders 2001). Amortized variational inference over interaction
graphs (Kipf et al. 2018) and differentiable relaxations of discrete
structure (Mena et al. 2018) make such models trainable by gradient
descent. Our covariate term plays the role of degree correction or
fitness, as in Peixoto (2020).

\textbf{Reconstruction as Bayesian inference.} A separate line infers a
network from noisy or partial reports of its edges (Guimerà and
Sales-Pardo 2009; Martin, Ball, and Newman 2016; Peixoto 2018, 2019).
Our setting gives no edge-level signal at all: only row and column sums.
These methods are therefore close in spirit but not drop-in baselines;
adapting them to margin-only input is outside our scope.

\textbf{Model selection versus recovery.} Vallès-Català et al. (2018)
show that a model's own selection criterion and its link-recovery
performance can disagree. Our real-data stability comparison has the
same problem, since the model is trained to be smooth (Section 6.5).

\subsection{3.2 Generative model}\label{generative-model}

\begin{flalign}
    z_i &\sim \text{Categorical}(\pi) && \label{eq:latent_z} \\
    \phi_{i,t} &= \exp(w^\top X_{i,t}) && \label{eq:latent_phi} \\
    \lambda_{ij,t} &= \Lambda_{z_i z_j}\,\phi_{i,t}\,\phi_{j,t} && \label{eq:expected_flows} \\
    \Pr(\tilde W_{ij,t} = 0) &= \sigma(-\eta_{ij,t}), \quad \tilde W_{ij,t} \mid \tilde W_{ij,t} > 0 
    \sim \text{LogNormal}(\log \lambda_{ij,t}, \sigma^2) && \label{eq:zero_inflated_lognormal} \\
    r_{i,t} &= \sum_j W_{ij,t}, \quad c_{j,t} = \sum_i W_{ij,t} && \label{eq:marginal_sums}
\end{flalign}

The zero inflation in (Equation \ref{eq:zero_inflated_lognormal})
reflects that most bank pairs have no exposure. Because (Equation
\ref{eq:marginal_sums}) is a deterministic constraint, not a noisy
observation, we enforce it in the architecture through a projection
(Section 3.3) rather than in the likelihood. \(\Lambda\), \(w\) and the
block-level existence logits are parameters of the synthetic generator
only; the trained model replaces (Equation
\ref{eq:expected_flows})--(Equation \ref{eq:zero_inflated_lognormal})
with a learned decoder.

\subsection{3.3 Capped projection}\label{capped-projection}

Given a raw matrix \(\tilde W_t\), the output is its Euclidean
projection onto the intersection of the row-sum set \(\mathcal{R}\), the
column-sum set \(\mathcal{C}\) and the box \(\mathcal{B}\):

\begin{flalign}
    W_t = \text{Proj}_{\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{B}}(\tilde W_t), \quad
    \mathcal{B} = \{W : 0 \le W_{ij} \le \kappa K_{i,t}\ \forall j\} && \tag{8} \label{eq:capped_projection}
\end{flalign}

The box caps each bilateral exposure at a fraction \(\kappa\) of the
lender's capital fund, the form of a single-obligor limit. We use
\(\kappa = 0.05\) (TO VERIFY against \textbf{nrbunified?}) .

We compute (Equation \ref{eq:capped_projection}) with Dykstra's
alternating projections (Boyle and Dykstra 1986) (Algorithm
\ref{alg:capacitated_projection} )\footnote{For details refer Boyle and
  Dykstra (1986) and Tibshirani (2017)}. Each of the three set
projections is closed form: a uniform shift of each row, a uniform shift
of each column, and a clip. The iteration converges to the exact
projection when the intersection is non-empty, and gradients pass
through it by automatic differentiation (the clip has the usual
subgradient). When the intersection is empty the iteration returns a
least-violation point that keeps the box but misses the margins. We
treat that case as a property of the inputs to be reported, not as a
solution; on real data it is the usual case (Section 6.2).

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{Capacitated Projection}
  \label{alg:capacitated_projection}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require $\tilde{W}$ (initial matrix), $r$ (target row sums), $c$ (target column sums), $\text{cap}$ (capacity limits), $n_{\text{iters}}$, $\text{tol}$
      \Ensure $W$ (projected matrix)
      \State $W \leftarrow \tilde{W}$; \quad $I_{\text{row}}, I_{\text{col}}, I_{\text{box}} \leftarrow 0$
      \For{$\text{iter} = 1, \dots, n_{\text{iters}}$}
          \State $W_{\text{prev}} \leftarrow W$
          \State $Y \leftarrow W + I_{\text{row}}; \quad W \leftarrow Y + \frac{r - \text{rowsums}(Y)}{N}; \quad I_{\text{row}} \leftarrow Y - W$
          \State $Y \leftarrow W + I_{\text{col}}; \quad W \leftarrow Y + \frac{c - \text{colsums}(Y)}{N}; \quad I_{\text{col}} \leftarrow Y - W$
          \State $Y \leftarrow W + I_{\text{box}}; \quad W \leftarrow \text{clip}(Y, 0, \text{cap}_i) \text{ for every entry of row } i; \quad I_{\text{box}} \leftarrow Y - W$
          \If{$\|W - W_{\text{prev}}\| < \text{tol}$} 
              \State \textbf{break}
          \EndIf
      \EndFor
      \State \textbf{return} $W$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{3.4 A pitfall: capping rows instead of
entries}\label{a-pitfall-capping-rows-instead-of-entries}

A natural first attempt adds a cap to Sinkhorn scaling by clipping the
row scalar \(u_i\). A row scalar rescales a whole row, so the clip
enforces \(\sum_j W_{ij} \le \kappa K_{i,t}\), a limit on total
interbank lending, instead of \(W_{ij} \le \kappa K_{i,t}\) for each
counterparty, a limit on concentration. A unit test with a binding
per-entry cap exposed the difference. Plain, uncapped Sinkhorn remains
our RAS baseline (Cuturi 2013; Gandy and Veraart 2013).

\subsection{3.5 Inference network}\label{inference-network}

A GRU encodes each bank's history,
\(h_{i,t} = \text{GRU}_\theta([X_{i,t}; r_{i,t}; c_{i,t}],
h_{i,t-1})\) (6). The marginals enter the encoder because in (1)--(2)
the block is drawn independently of the covariates, so covariates alone
carry no information about \(z\); the marginals do, through \(\Lambda\).
A small network gives
\(q_\psi(z_i) = \text{softmax}(\text{MLP}_\psi(h_{i,t}))\) (7), and a
pairwise decoder maps \((h_{i,t}, h_{j,t}, z_i, z_j)\) to
\((\eta_{ij,t}, \lambda_{ij,t})\). Training samples use the
Gumbel-softmax, Gumbel-sigmoid and log-normal reparameterizations; the
reported \(\hat W_t\) uses posterior means (\(q_\psi(z)\),
\(\sigma(\eta)\) and \(\lambda e^{\sigma^2/2}\)) before projection,
which removes sampling noise from the point estimate. We use a GRU
rather than a temporal graph network because the panel is small (16
banks).

\subsection{3.6 Training objective}\label{training-objective}

\begin{flalign}
    \mathcal{L} &= \underbrace{-\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(W_t^{\text{true}})]}_{\text{synthetic only}} +
    \beta_t\,\text{KL}\big(q_\psi(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big) + \lambda_{\text{smooth}} \sum_t \|W_t -
    W_{t-1}\|_F^2 && \tag{9} \label{eq:loss_function}
\end{flalign}

\(\beta_t\) rises linearly to \(\beta_{\max} = 0.15\) over 300 epochs,
with a free-bits floor of 0.05 per bank and period, and
\(\lambda_{\text{smooth}} = 0.05\). The code also contains a
mutual-information clustering term; a sweep over its weight (Appendix C)
found no benefit, and it is set to zero in every reported run.

On real data the first term of (Equation \ref{eq:loss_function}) is
identically zero, because no bilateral ground truth exists. The KL term
depends only on \(q_\psi(z)\) and gives the decoder no gradient. The
only term that reaches the decoder is the smoothness penalty on the
projected matrices. Real-data training is therefore a regularized fit to
the margins, not likelihood estimation, and Section 6.4 shows its
consequence.

\section{4. Data}\label{data}

\textbf{Source.} NRB's monthly \emph{Banking and Financial Statistics}
bulletins, published as PDFs. We extract each commercial bank's balance
sheet. The two margins are sub-items of the balance sheet: \(r_{i,t}\)
is \texttt{a.\ Interbank\ Lending} under
\texttt{3\ SHARE\ \&\ OTHER\ INVESTMENT} (assets), and \(c_{i,t}\) is
\texttt{b.\ Interbank\ Borrowing} under \texttt{2\ BORROWINGS}
(liabilities). \(K_{i,t}\) is \texttt{1\ CAPITAL\ FUND}. The five
covariates are size (the sum of the ten top-level asset rows), the
capital and liquidity ratios, loans over deposits, and NRB borrowing
over total borrowing (Appendix B).

\textbf{Extraction.} Item numbers drift across eras, lettered sub-items
repeat under different parents, early bulletins lack a liabilities
total, and rows are interleaved across banks. The extractor keys on
normalized label text and parent section, tracks the balance-sheet side
per bank, and treats an absent optional row as zero only where it is a
component of a sum. An audit of the accounting identities (Paper 2,
Section 4.2) finds 96--100\% of sheets passing through Falgun 2075 and a
sharp collapse from Chaitra 2075; we call the months through Falgun 2075
the clean window. We have not traced the later failures to the PDFs
rather than to the extraction.

\textbf{Complete-case rule.} We never impute. A bank enters only if
every field is present in every month of the modelling window.
Missingness here has identifiable causes (an era's numbering, a bank
absent from a page) and is not missing completely at random, so
imputation would inject bias tied to the failure modes
(\textbf{littlerubin2019missing?}). The rule leaves N = 16 of 33
commercial-bank labels over T = 133 months, Asar 2072 -- Shrawan 2083.
One calendar month, Kartik 2078, is absent. Section 6.1 reports who is
excluded and why.

\section{5. Synthetic results}\label{synthetic-results}

\subsection{5.1 Design}\label{design}

The generator (Appendix A, Algorithm 2) draws 24 banks over 16 periods
with \(K = 2\) blocks, \(\Lambda = ([2.5, 0.6], [0.6, 0.2])\) block
existence logits \(\Lambda = ([2, 0], [0, -2])\) five covariates five
covariates with weights \(w = (0.05, 0.8, 0.5, -0.3, 0.4)\),
\(\sigma^2 = 0.15\) and cap 6.0. The ground truth is itself projected
through Algorithm 1, so it satisfies the cap the model must satisfy; RAS
does not use the cap, an asymmetry that favours our method and that the
capped-RAS check in Section 5.4 would isolate. We train for 3,000 epochs
on seeds 0--4 and compare (i) the relative Frobenius error of
\(\hat W_t\) against RAS on the same margins and (ii) block accuracy
against the majority-class guess (0.70).

\subsection{5.2 Reconstruction}\label{reconstruction}

The model's mean relative error is lower than RAS's in 5/5 seeds (0.430
± 0.043 vs.~0.566 ± 0.026); a paired t-test across seeds gives p =
0.0101. (With five seeds, Wilcoxon's smallest attainable two-sided p is
0.0625, so we report the t-test.) A capped-RAS baseline --- plain
Sinkhorn given the same per-pair cap the method uses (Section 5.4) ---
lets us split that gain into its two sources. Of the 0.136 total
improvement over RAS, the cap supplies 0.101 (RAS 0.566 → capped RAS
0.465, paired p = 0.028) and the covariate-informed learned prior
supplies the remaining 0.036 (capped RAS 0.465 → model 0.430), the
latter small but consistent across all five seeds (paired p = 0.0011).
(The two components are computed from the unrounded seed means and so
need not equal the differences of the three-digit figures shown here.)
About three-quarters of the win is therefore the regulatory cap and
one-quarter the learned prior; and that learned-prior quarter is not the
block structure. A single-block (\(K=1\)) ablation leaves reconstruction
error statistically unchanged (0.420 vs.~0.430, paired p = 0.36; Section
5.4) --- and still below capped RAS --- so the gain beyond the cap comes
from the covariates, the temporal encoder and the learned decoder, not
from the covariate-informed blockmodel.

\subsection{5.3 Block recovery}\label{block-recovery}

Block accuracy exceeds the majority guess by 0.0781 on average (sd
0.0774), in 3 of 5 seeds (paired t-test p = 0.1136). The margin is
smaller than its spread, and two failure modes recur across
configurations: collapse to the uniform prior, and confident but wrong
assignments. We therefore do not claim that the model identifies core
and periphery banks. Table C1 shows that the mutual-information term
does not change this at any weight tried.

\subsection{5.4 What the synthetic win is and is
not}\label{what-the-synthetic-win-is-and-is-not}

The synthetic ground truth comes from the same blockmodel the method
assumes, which favours the method. The three checks this invites are now
run. The capped-RAS baseline (Section 5.2) attributes about
three-quarters of the gain over RAS to the per-pair cap and one-quarter
to the learned prior. A single-block ablation (\(K=1\)) then isolates
what the block channel itself contributes: with the number of blocks cut
from two to one, reconstruction error is statistically unchanged (0.420
at \(K=1\) vs.~0.430 at \(K=2\), paired t-test p = 0.36, and still below
the 0.465 of capped RAS), while block accuracy collapses mechanically to
a single block. Since the block labels are not recovered in the first
place (Section 5.3), this says the covariate-informed blockmodel adds
nothing to reconstruction beyond the cap, the smoothness prior, the
temporal encoder and the learned decoder: the learned-prior quarter of
the gain is those components, not the communities. A misspecification
check completes the picture. When the generator has no block structure
(homogeneous blocks --- a pure covariate/degree fitness model), the
method still beats RAS in all five seeds, but by far less: model 0.730
vs.~RAS 0.759, a mean gap of 0.029 against the 0.136 it has on
block-structured data. Block accuracy sits exactly at the trivial
majority guess (0.524 vs.~0.522, gap 0.002), so the model does not
invent communities that are not there. Imposing the blockmodel therefore
does not hurt under misspecification: the reconstruction advantage
shrinks toward the cap-only margin and the latent structure stays inert,
consistent with Sections 5.2--5.3.

A persistence baseline is reported on the real panel in Section 6.5; on
the synthetic panel it is uninformative, because the generator redraws
blocks and covariates independently each period, so consecutive \(W_t\)
carry no temporal dependence for a persistence rule to exploit.

\section{6. Real-data results}\label{real-data-results}

\subsection{6.1 The panel and who is
missing}\label{the-panel-and-who-is-missing}

The 16 banks are ADBNL, EBL, Global, HBL, Kumari, Laxmi, MBL, NABIL,
NBL, NIC, NMB, NSBI, Prime, RBB, SBL and SCBNL. Three facts limit what
the panel represents. - \textbf{It is a closed system.} Exposures to the
other commercial banks, development banks, NRB and foreign banks are
outside the matrix. - \textbf{The discard is not size-neutral.} Excluded
banks are smaller (pre-window size medians 64,910 NPR mn included
vs.~44,510 excluded; Mann--Whitney p = 0.005). - \textbf{Some exclusions
come from later months.} Over the clean window alone (45 months), 21
labels are complete. BOK (first incomplete month Poush 2079), NCC (Poush
2079), NIBL (Magh 2079), Sunrise (Asar 2080) and Mega (Asar 2076) are
dropped only because the filter runs over all 133 months. Why these
series stop has not been verified. - \textbf{Some exclusions may be
relabels.} Citizens→Citizen, NBB→NBBL, PRABHU→Prabhu and Civil→CBL each
switch label at Poush/Magh 2075 with no overlap and size and capital
ratios of 0.969--1.042 across the switch. Each half fails the
complete-case rule. We have not checked these against NRB records, so
``33'' counts labels; there are at most 29 distinct banks if all four
are relabels.

\subsection{6.2 The published margins do not
balance}\label{the-published-margins-do-not-balance}

In the clean window (45 months, 720 bank-months), lending is zero in 398
bank-months and borrowing in 465. Within the 16 banks, total lending and
total borrowing differ by more than 1\% in 43 of 45 months and by more
than 10\% in 37; the median relative gap is 0.32 and the maximum 0.93
(Mangsir 2072: lending NPR 10,322 mn vs.~borrowing NPR 743 mn). In 16
bank-months one bank lends more than all the others borrow, and in 2 one
bank borrows more than all the others lend. A non-negative matrix with
zero diagonal must have equal totals and must not let one row exceed the
other columns' total, so in 43 of 45 months no such matrix matches both
margins, before the cap is considered.

Within-panel imbalance is expected whenever the 16 banks are not a
closed system: a panel bank's lending total counts loans to banks
outside the panel, whose matching borrowing is not in the panel's column
totals, so the two panel totals need not be equal even if every figure
is correct. This is the leading explanation. A difference in how the two
bulletin lines are defined would also produce it, and we cannot say from
the published aggregates how much of the gap is open-system leakage and
how much, if any, is definitional; we have not checked the line
definitions with NRB.

\subsection{6.3 What the projection does with infeasible
margins}\label{what-the-projection-does-with-infeasible-margins}

With an empty intersection, Algorithm 1 returns its least-violation
point, and that is the \(\hat W_t\) we report. Its worst monthly miss
tracks the gap (Spearman 0.93): the median worst miss is NPR 200.8 mn in
the 43 infeasible months and 22.28 mn in the other 2. The largest misses
are NPR 2,311 mn on lending and 437.8 mn on borrowing. The projection
does not keep zeros: \(\hat W_t\) assigns more than NPR 1 mn of
borrowing to all 465 bank-months that report none (16 of 16 banks;
median 100.9, maximum 437.8) and more than NPR 1 mn of lending to 249 of
the 398 that report none (13 banks; median 27.1, maximum 833.4).

RAS fits the same margins differently. It rescales entries, so it keeps
every zero, matches lending to within NPR 41 mn, and leaves the whole
gap on borrowing (deviations up to NPR 8,290 mn). Both methods' worst
misses track the gap (Spearman 0.93 for each, Paper 2 Section 8). The
size of the misfit is therefore a property of the published data; how it
is split is a property of each method. The two networks are not
independent estimates: they share their targets, and a downstream
exposure measure built from them correlates 0.968 over the companion
paper's post-mandate cells.

The cap rarely binds: 7.0\% of entries over all 133 months and 1.9\% in
the clean window (\(\kappa = 0.05\)). On real data the cap adds little
information beyond the margins; its role rests on the synthetic results.

\subsection{6.4 The model's latent structure does not reach the
output}\label{the-models-latent-structure-does-not-reach-the-output}

We ran two fits that differ only in the KL weight. With
\(\beta_{\max} = 0.15\) every bank's posterior is near uniform (about
0.45/0.55); with \(\beta_{\max} = 0\) the KL term saturates at 0.691
(\(\approx \ln 2\)) and all 16 banks sit at \(\ge 0.999\) on the same
block. The two posteriors are at opposite degenerate ends, yet the two
\(\hat W_t\) series differ by a relative Frobenius difference of
\(1.5\times10^{-5}\) (maximum absolute difference NPR 0.18 mn). On a
matched synthetic pair, where the reconstruction term is active, a much
smaller posterior change (mean absolute difference 0.044) moves
\(\hat W_t\) by a relative difference of 0.285.

This follows from Section 3.6. Without ground truth, no loss term
rewards any particular matrix among those near the margins, and the
decoder receives gradient only through the smoothness penalty. On real
data, \(\hat W_t\) is the projection of a smooth, nearly arbitrary raw
matrix onto inconsistent margins, and the blockmodel and covariates do
not detectably shape it.

\subsection{6.5 Temporal stability}\label{temporal-stability}

\(\hat W_t\) changes less from month to month than RAS: the mean
month-to-month change of \(\hat W_t\) is 1.16 against RAS's 2.32 (lower
is more stable; seed 0, 16 banks over 133 months). But this edge is not
evidence that the model recovers stable structure. A persistence
baseline makes the point: carrying last month's reconstruction forward
and re-imposing the current month's margins and cap through Algorithm 1
already gives a month-to-month change of 1.11 --- essentially the
model's own 1.16. The model departs from that carried-forward matrix by
only 13\%, so it adds almost nothing beyond persistence plus
marginal-fitting. The gap to RAS therefore reflects that RAS over-reacts
to month-to-month noise in the published margins, not that the model's
smoothness prior or latent structure contributes stability of its own
(Vallès-Català et al. 2018). This is consistent with Section 6.4: on
real data the decoder receives gradient only through the smoothness
penalty.

\section{7. Discussion and
limitations}\label{discussion-and-limitations}

\textbf{What the method offers.} Where margins are consistent, the
capped projection gives the nearest matrix that matches the totals and
respects a per-counterparty limit, and the synthetic results show that
the learned prior improves on RAS when there is a signal to learn. The
projection is a standalone, differentiable layer that other
reconstruction models can use.

\textbf{What Nepal's bulletins allow.} Three findings bound any
reconstruction from these data, not only ours. First, the two published
lending and borrowing totals do not balance within the observed banks in
43 of 45 clean months --- most likely because the panel is not a closed
system, possibly also because the two lines are defined differently ---
so an exact bilateral reconstruction restricted to the panel does not
exist and every method must choose where to put the misfit. Second, most
bank-months report zero, so the margins carry little information about
who trades with whom. Third, without bilateral ground truth, nothing in
the data selects one matrix over another near the margins; our model's
latent structure has no detectable effect on its output. For NRB, the
practical implication is that bilateral or large-exposure reporting,
even at low frequency, would be needed to make interbank exposure maps
informative; reconciling the definitions of the two published interbank
lines would be a cheaper first step.

\textbf{Limitations.} - \emph{Ground truth.} All accuracy evidence is
synthetic and from the assumed model class. The capped-RAS decomposition
(Section 5.2), the single-block (\(K=1\)) ablation and the non-SBM
misspecification check (Section 5.4), and the real-data persistence
baseline (Section 6.5) are all now run. Even where bilateral data exist,
reconstructions differ substantially from them (Mistrulli 2011). -
\emph{Block identity.} Not recovered on synthetic data and not
identifiable on real data; we make no claim about it. - \emph{Sample.} A
closed system of 16 larger banks; the complete-case rule over the full
span drops five banks that are complete in the clean window, and four
possible relabels are unresolved. - \emph{Training window.} The model is
trained on all 133 months, including bulletins whose line items fail the
accounting identities from Chaitra 2075. Each month's matrix is fitted
to that month's margins, but the smoothness penalty links Falgun 2075 to
Chaitra 2075. A run on the clean window only would remove this and could
raise N to 21; a larger panel would also show how much of the Section
6.2 imbalance is open-system leakage rather than definitional. It is not
done. - \emph{Single seed on real data.} Seed sensitivity of the
real-data \(\hat W_t\) is not measured, and reconstruction uncertainty
is not propagated to downstream uses. - \emph{Model size.} The
reconstruction network has 2,133 trainable parameters (GRU encoder
1,200; block-posterior MLP 306; pairwise decoder 626; one scalar
dispersion), fixed entirely by the architecture (\(d=5\) covariates,
hidden width 16, \(K=2\) blocks) and independent of panel size --- the
same network serves the synthetic (\(N=24\), \(T=16\)) and real
(\(N=16\), \(T=133\)) panels. On the real panel this is comparable to
\(N \times T = 2{,}128\) and smaller than the \(2NT = 4{,}256\) observed
marginals, and about two orders of magnitude below the supervised
synthetic target entries used in training. The network is therefore
under- rather than over-parameterized: amortization replaces the
\(N(N-1)T \approx 3.2 \times 10^4\) per-period latent unknowns a
non-amortized reconstruction would carry with
\(\approx 2.1 \times 10^3\) shared weights, a \(>10\times\) reduction in
effective degrees of freedom. The binding constraint is not model
capacity but identifiability of the bilateral matrix from its margins
(Section 6.4), which no parameter budget resolves.

\section{8. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

We give a differentiable, capped projection for interbank reconstruction
and embed it in a covariate-informed blockmodel. On synthetic data the
combination reconstructs better than RAS but does not recover blocks. On
Nepal's bulletins the published lending and borrowing totals do not
balance within the observed banks, the projection's misfit tracks that
imbalance, and the model's latent structure leaves the output unchanged.
The result is a measured data limit: the published marginals cannot say
who is exposed to whom, and we state what reporting would be needed to
change that.

\section{Appendix A --- Algorithms 2 and
3}\label{appendix-a-algorithms-2-and-3}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Sample Synthetic Network}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require $N, K, \pi, \Lambda, H, w, \sigma^2, X, \kappa, K_{\text{cap}}$
  \Ensure $W_{\text{true}}, \text{rowsums}(W_{\text{true}}), \text{colsums}(W_{\text{true}})$
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, N$}
      \State $z_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi); \quad \phi_i \leftarrow \exp(w^\top X_i)$
  \EndFor
  \For{$i \neq j$}
      \State $\lambda_{ij} \leftarrow \Lambda_{z_i, z_j} \, \phi_i \, \phi_j$
      \State $e_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\sigma(H_{z_i, z_j}))$
      \State $\tilde{W}_{ij} \leftarrow e_{ij} \cdot \text{LogNormal}(\log \lambda_{ij}, \sigma^2)$
  \EndFor
  \State $W_{\text{true}} \leftarrow \text{CapacitatedProjection}\big(\tilde{W}, \text{rowsums}(\tilde{W}), \text{colsums}(\tilde{W}), \kappa \cdot K_{\text{cap}}\big)$
  \State \textbf{return} $W_{\text{true}}, \text{rowsums}(W_{\text{true}}), \text{colsums}(W_{\text{true}})$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Training Step}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require Batch $\{X_t, r_t, c_t, K_t\}$, optional $W_{\text{true}, t}$
  \For{$t$ in temporal order}
      \State $h_{i,t} \leftarrow \text{GRU}\left([X_{i,t}; r_{i,t}; c_{i,t}], \, h_{i,t-1}\right)$
      \State $q_i \leftarrow \text{softmax}\big(\text{MLP}(h_{i,t})\big)$
      \State $z_i \leftarrow \text{GumbelSoftmax}(q_i)$
      \State $(\eta_{ij,t}, \lambda_{ij,t}) \leftarrow \text{Decoder}(h_{i,t}, h_{j,t}, z_i, z_j)$
      \State $\tilde{W}_{ij,t} \leftarrow \text{GumbelSigmoid}(\eta_{ij,t}) \cdot \text{LogNormal}(\log \lambda_{ij,t}, \sigma^2)$
      \State $W_t \leftarrow \text{CapacitatedProjection}(\tilde{W}_t, r_t, c_t, \kappa \cdot K_t)$
      \If{$W_{\text{true}, t}$ is given}
          \State $L_{\text{recon}, t} \leftarrow -\log p(W_{\text{true}, t} \mid \lambda_t, \eta_t, \sigma^2)$
      \Else
          \State $L_{\text{recon}, t} \leftarrow 0$
      \EndIf
      \State $L_{\text{KL}, t} \leftarrow \text{KL}\big(q(z_t) \,\|\, p(z)\big)$
  \EndFor
  \State $L \leftarrow \sum_t \left(L_{\text{recon}, t} + \beta \cdot L_{\text{KL}, t}\right) + \lambda_{\text{smooth}} \sum_t \|W_t - W_{t-1}\|_F^2$
  \State backpropagate through Algorithm 1; optimizer step
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
